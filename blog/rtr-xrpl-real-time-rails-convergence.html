Payments Canada's RTR and XRPL: Why Real-Time Rails Are a Convergence Moment

Canada's payments infrastructure is on the verge of a major shift. The Real-Time Rail, originally planned for a 2022 launch, has been delayed multiple times and is now targeting industry testing in 2026, with a likely launch in late 2026 or early 2027. When it arrives, Canadian financial institutions will finally have a domestic real-time payment system capable of settling transactions in seconds around the clock. At the same time, the XRP Ledger has been operating globally with 3–5 second settlement finality since its inception, and added native automated market maker functionality on March 22, 2024. The convergence of these two systems presents a specific question for Canadian fintechs: what would it take to bridge between a regulated national rail and a permissionless global ledger, and why would they bother?

The answer lies in understanding how these systems work, where they differ, and what capabilities emerge when institutions can move value across both.

Speed and Settlement: Different Architectures, Similar Outcomes

The Real-Time Rail operates as a real-time gross settlement system. Once a payment is initiated, it must be exchanged, cleared, and settled between financial institutions within 10 seconds, then made available to the recipient within 60 seconds. Payments are irrevocable and offer immediate settlement finality. The system runs 24/7/365 and uses the ISO 20022 messaging standard for all transactions. To send a payment, participants must maintain sufficient funds in their settlement account at the Bank of Canada. If the account lacks sufficient funds, the payment is rejected.

The XRP Ledger settles transactions in 3–5 seconds with finality. It uses a consensus protocol in which validators work together to agree on the order and validity of transactions. Once a transaction appears in a validated ledger with a tesSUCCESS result code, it is final and cannot be reversed. The ledger operates continuously, and as long as fewer than 20% of trusted validators are faulty, consensus continues unimpeded. Confirming an invalid transaction would require over 80% of trusted validators to collude.

Both systems deliver what their names promise: real-time settlement with finality. The RTR achieves this through centralized infrastructure managed by Payments Canada, with the Bank of Canada providing settlement accounts. XRPL achieves it through a decentralized network of validators running the same protocol. The operational result is similar. The mechanism is entirely different.

Liquidity: Pre-Funding vs. On-Chain Markets

The Real-Time Rail requires participants to pre-fund settlement accounts. A direct settlement participant holds its own Bank of Canada settlement account and settles payments in real time. An indirect settlement participant uses a settlement agent, which is a direct participant that settles on behalf of others. Either way, liquidity must be in place before a payment can execute. This is a gross settlement model: each payment settles individually, and rejected payments do not enter the system.

The XRP Ledger provides liquidity through its decentralized exchange and, since March 22, 2024, through on-chain automated market makers. An AMM holds a pool of two assets and sets exchange rates based on the balance in the pool. When a user trades against the AMM, the exchange rate adjusts according to a constant product formula. Liquidity providers deposit assets into AMMs and receive LP tokens in return, earning returns as others trade against the pool. The DEX also supports a traditional order book model, where users can place limit and market orders for any token pair.

For a Canadian fintech operating on both rails, this difference matters. On the RTR, the institution must hold Canadian dollar liquidity in a settlement account. On XRPL, liquidity can be provided by third parties through AMMs or accessed via the DEX. The RTR model guarantees finality but requires capital commitment. The XRPL model offers access to global liquidity pools but introduces exchange rate risk and potential slippage depending on pool depth.

Access: Membership vs. Permissionless Participation

Participation in the Real-Time Rail requires regulatory approval and Payments Canada membership. Payment service providers must first register with the Bank of Canada under the Retail Payment Activities Act, then apply for Payments Canada membership and RTR participation. Applicants must meet operational and technical requirements, including compliance with ISO 20022 messaging standards and specific RTR payment message formats outlined in the Participation Guide published on August 21, 2025. Participants can connect directly to the RTR exchange or use a connection service provider. They can settle directly or through a settlement agent. Either way, entry requires approval from both Payments Canada and the Bank of Canada.

The XRP Ledger is permissionless. Anyone can create an account, submit transactions, run a validator, or provide liquidity to an AMM. There are no membership requirements, no registration processes, and no approval gates. The protocol enforces rules through code, and participation is open to any entity willing to follow those rules.

For a Canadian fintech, this creates a clear regulatory divide. Operating on the RTR requires navigating the Payments Canada membership process, maintaining settlement accounts, and complying with ongoing operational standards. Operating on XRPL requires technical integration with the ledger and compliance with any applicable Canadian regulations governing digital asset transactions, but the ledger itself imposes no entry barrier.

Bridging the Two: Technical and Regulatory Requirements

Bridging between the RTR and XRPL would require simultaneous participation in both systems. On the RTR side, this means obtaining Payments Canada membership, maintaining a settlement account, and integrating with the RTR exchange using ISO 20022 messaging. On the XRPL side, this means running infrastructure capable of submitting and monitoring transactions on the ledger, managing private keys securely, and potentially operating as a liquidity provider on the DEX or within AMM pools.

The technical integration point is message translation and liquidity management. An RTR payment arrives as an ISO 20022 message and settles in Canadian dollars. To move that value onto XRPL, the institution would need to issue a corresponding token on the ledger, maintain reserves to back that token, and provide liquidity so others can trade it. The reverse flow — receiving value on XRPL and settling it via RTR — requires monitoring XRPL transactions, validating receipt of tokens, then initiating an RTR payment to the final recipient.

Regulatory compliance introduces additional layers. The institution operating the bridge would likely be subject to anti-money laundering obligations under Canadian law, including customer identification, transaction monitoring, and reporting requirements. It would need to ensure that both RTR and XRPL flows comply with applicable sanctions and financial crime controls. And it would need to manage operational risk, including the possibility of downtime on either system, transaction failures, or discrepancies between ledgers.

No Canadian institution currently operates such a bridge. The RTR has not yet launched, and XRPL adoption in Canada remains limited to specific use cases. But the technical path is clear, and the regulatory framework is becoming more defined as payment service providers gain access to Payments Canada membership under amendments to the Canadian Payments Act expected in Fall 2025.

Why It Matters

The case for bridging these systems rests on what each can do that the other cannot. The RTR provides access to the Canadian banking system with finality and regulatory clarity. XRPL provides access to global liquidity, programmable assets, and 24/7 settlement without pre-funded accounts. A fintech that can move value between them can offer services that neither system alone supports: instant cross-border settlement using Canadian dollars, on-chain liquidity for Canadian payment flows, or programmable payment logic that settles into regulated bank accounts.

The Canada angle is structural. Canadian businesses and financial institutions operate within a domestic payments ecosystem that has historically been slower and more fragmented than systems in markets like the U.K., Europe, or Australia. The RTR is Canada's answer to that gap, arriving later than most comparable jurisdictions but with the advantage of learning from their implementations. XRPL offers a parallel infrastructure that does not require waiting for national deployment timelines or regulatory approval cycles. The convergence of the two creates optionality for institutions willing to navigate both worlds.

Whether that optionality translates into adoption depends on demand. If Canadian fintechs see a market for services that require both regulated domestic settlement and global on-chain liquidity, the bridge becomes valuable. If demand remains siloed — domestic payments on the RTR, digital assets on XRPL, with no meaningful overlap — the complexity of bridging may not justify the effort. The RTR's rollout through 2026 and into 2027 will clarify which scenario prevails.
